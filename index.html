Milan Vojnović is Professor of Data Science and Head of the Department of Statistics at the London School of Economics. He develops algorithms, with supporting theory, for reliable and efficient machine learning in large-scale AI systems. Before joining LSE, he spent twelve years at Microsoft Research, and between 2019 and 2026 he was a Visiting Scientist at Meta, where joint work has been deployed in production.

His recent research covers post-training and alignment of language models, including what reinforcement-learning methods such as GRPO actually optimise and how preferences are aggregated when multiple agents learn from one another; reliability and calibration of model predictions across many overlapping subpopulations; learning from limited feedback, where information is costly to acquire, through active learning, bandits, and sequential decision-making; and scalable optimisation for distributed and communication-efficient training. A common thread is understanding what can be learned when information, computation, or feedback is constrained, and when the behaviour of the system or its users shapes the data it learns from.

Current research

Post-training and alignment

What do alignment methods actually optimise, and whose preferences do they reflect?

Reinforcement-learning methods such as GRPO are widely used to post-train language models, but what they optimise is not always explicit. What is the Alignment Objective of GRPO? characterises the objective that Group Relative Policy Optimisation optimises and how it relates to the reading of GRPO as an alignment method. Aggregation Rules Induced by KL-Regularised Preference Dynamics on Networks studies agents that each update a KL-regularised policy, as in RLHF or Nash learning from human feedback (NLHF), while observing one another, and identifies the preference-aggregation rule this collectively produces, depending on how agents combine what they observe.

Reliability and calibration

How can predictions be made reliable across many different populations?

A model can be well calibrated on average while being systematically over- or under-confident for particular groups of users or inputs, which matters whenever a model’s confidence is used to make decisions. Multicalibration asks for calibration across many overlapping subpopulations at once. MCGrad makes this practical at scale: it automatically finds the subpopulations where calibration can be improved and has been deployed across hundreds of production models at Meta (code at mcgrad.dev). A companion paper, On the Convergence of Multicalibration Gradient Boosting, establishes when and how fast the procedure converges.

Learning from limited feedback

How should an algorithm learn when information is costly or incomplete?

Labels, human judgements, and experiments are expensive, so deciding what to query is often as important as how to learn from it. This work approaches the question through bandits, active learning, and sequential decision-making. Oracle-Efficient Combinatorial Semi-Bandits develops combinatorial semi-bandit algorithms that reduce the number of calls to the optimisation oracle while maintaining regret guarantees. On the Convergence of Loss and Uncertainty-based Active Learning Algorithms analyses active-learning methods that select training points by their loss or predictive uncertainty. Related work studies infinitely-many-armed bandits whose rewards deteriorate as they are played (ICML 2022; NeurIPS 2024).

Scalable optimisation

How can machine learning scale when computation and communication are constrained?

Learning at scale is limited by compute, memory, and communication, and this work develops methods that exploit problem structure to reduce these costs. GL-LowPopArt exploits low-rank structure to give a nearly instance-optimal estimator for generalised low-rank trace regression, and Sketching Stochastic Valuation Functions studies how compact sketches of stochastic valuation functions support efficient optimisation. This line of work goes back to QSGD, which introduced gradient quantisation to cut communication in distributed training.

Research in production

Several of his methods have been transferred into large-scale industry systems, at Microsoft and at Meta. He spent twelve years at Microsoft Research, most recently as Senior Researcher, and held three Visiting Scientist appointments at Meta between 2019 and 2026, in the Central Applied Science and Core Data Science teams. He is a named inventor on twelve granted US patents.

His work focuses on problems that come from the constraints of real systems: limited feedback, compute and communication budgets, heterogeneous user populations, and changing data.

Earlier research

Earlier work spans networking and distributed systems, consensus and information dissemination, large-scale graph partitioning, game theory, contests, and crowdsourcing. A long-running thread is inferring rankings from comparisons, from SerialRank (NeurIPS 2014) and generalised Thurstone choice models (ICML 2016) to fast algorithms for Bradley–Terry models (AISTATS 2020; Operations Research 2022), the same models that underpin reward learning from human preferences.

These projects share a common theme with current research: understanding how to make decisions and learn from data when information is incomplete and other agents may influence the system.
